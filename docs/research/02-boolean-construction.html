Boolean shapes from skeletons

Five ways to build merges, cut-outs and clips from stroke skeletons. Each one is tested for whether the masters stay compatible and whether every weight still matches what the designer meant.

Follows research 01. Code: prototype/booleans.py in snds/variance.

The short answer. Don't run booleans on outlines. Turn each boolean into a rule on the skeleton.

Merges: leave the parts overlapping and let the font's fill merge them. That's exact and free.

Clips: make one part's edge follow another part's generated edge.

Cut-outs with a gap, like the slash in an "off" icon or the notch around a badge: trim the skeleton where the cutter crosses it, and shape the cut ends from the cutter.

All three stayed compatible on every test icon. The worst one was off from the true boolean by 0.33 px² in total, across the whole icon.

What "a rule on the skeleton" means

An outline boolean takes two finished shapes and works out where their edges cross. That answer is only right for one weight. At another weight the edges cross somewhere else, so the boolean builds a differently made shape. One weight might have 10 points and another 12. Masters built that way often can't interpolate.

A skeleton rule stores what you meant instead of the result. For example: "this slash cuts the ring, with 1 px of clearance each side." At every weight, the pipeline rebuilds the cut from that sentence, using a shape whose structure is decided in advance. Only the point positions change with weight, never how many points there are.

Worked example: the "off" slash

  1. Source: a ring skeleton (a circle) and a slash skeleton (a line) tagged cut gap=1.
  2. At each weight: work out the ring's outer and inner edges. Then work out two cut lines parallel to the slash, each half the stroke plus 1 px from its centre line.
  3. Build each half of the ring the same way every time: outer arc, straight cut edge, inner arc back, straight cut edge. That's always two pieces of six segments each.
  4. Add the slash as its own stroke.

The gap stays exactly 1 px at every weight, or follows whatever rule you give it, because the gap is part of the rule rather than something to redraw. The same idea covers the other cases. A merge's rule is "these parts overlap", so there's nothing to compute. A clip's rule is "this edge is that edge", so the half disc reuses the ring's inner-radius formula.

How the test works

Four test icons, each needing a different boolean. Each method builds three masters (weights 100, 400 and 700). The font then fills in the weights in between.

At nine weights, the result is compared with the true boolean, worked out from scratch at that weight. The score is the area where they disagree, in square pixels at 24 px. It's sampled every 0.1 px. Below about 0.5 px² the difference spreads along the edges and isn't visible.

The five methods

MethodHow it works
OverlapKeep every part as its own shape, overlapping. Fonts fill with the nonzero rule, so overlapping ink merges on screen. It can't make a gap.
Negative shapeCut by adding the cutter drawn backwards. Where it lies over ink, the ink cancels out.
Boolean per masterRun a real boolean (Skia, the engine behind Chrome) separately at each master weight. This is what flattening in Figma or Illustrator at each weight amounts to.
Boolean + repairThe same, then automatically reorder shapes and move start points to match the default master.

What each result means

Merges: overlap wins

Overlap was exact on the magnifier, with nothing to compute. Booleans per master also happened to work here, because merging never changed the shape's structure.

One trap. Overlapping parts must run the same direction. My first test had the handle wound the other way. Under the nonzero rule, opposite directions cancel, and the overlap rendered as a hole. SVGs from Figma and Illustrator can arrive either way, so the pipeline must set direction itself, from the part's role.

Overlaps are allowed in variable fonts, and Google's build tools flag them so renderers treat them correctly. If a static font is cut from the variable one, the overlaps should be merged at that step. fontTools' instancer has an option for this.

Clips: make one edge follow another

The contrast icon's half disc must end exactly at the ring's inner edge at every weight. Two constructions were exact. The half disc can share the formula for the ring's inner radius. Or it can overlap halfway into the ring's stroke, so the overlap hides the join. A clip is really a dependency: this edge follows that edge.

Cut-outs with a gap: only the skeleton cut works

These cut-outs are the hard case, and Material uses them everywhere: every "off" slash, and every badge.

  • Negative shapes failed badly: 32 to 84 px² wrong. They only work when the cutter lies entirely over ink. A gap strip runs past the ring into empty space, where "minus one layer" shows up as ink. On the badge, the dot over the negative shape cancelled into a hole.
  • Booleans per master broke compatibility on both. On the slash the shapes came out in a different order. On the badge, Skia split the curves at different places at the thin weight, so the point counts differed: 10 points against 12.
  • Repair fixed the slash but not the badge. Reordering and moving start points can't add missing points. It's also guesswork: it matches shapes by their centres, and that can pick wrongly on a busier icon.
  • The skeleton cut was compatible on both, at most 0.33 px² off. The point count is fixed by the construction, not by where curves happen to intersect.

Keeping design intent

The test shows the mechanism works. The bigger problem is getting the intent out of the design tool. As far as I know, Figma and Illustrator flatten boolean groups into one path when exporting SVG. By the time the pipeline sees the file, it can't tell a slash from a cut. It also can't tell how big the gap was meant to be.

So the cutter has to be marked in a way that survives export. Layer names can do this, because both tools can write layer names into the SVG as ids. A small naming grammar would cover every case above. This is a proposal to test, not a settled format:

Layer nameMeaning
slash cut gap=1Drawn, and also cuts every stroke it crosses, with 1 px clearance each side.
notch cut gap=1 hiddenCuts but isn't drawn. For notches with no visible cutter.
dot solidA filled part that gains a share of the weight change.
fill clip=ring.counterA part whose edge follows the ring's inner edge.
check knockoutWound opposite to its container, so it flips to a hole at FILL 1 (from research 01).

The alternative is a Figma plugin that reads live boolean groups and writes the construction out directly. That's more reliable inside Figma, but it would leave Illustrator and hand-written SVG behind. Layer names work everywhere, so I'd start there and add the plugin later.

Rules for the linter

  1. A cut has a valid weight range. If the gap plus half the stroke grows past the stroke it cuts, the piece disappears and the structure changes. The pipeline should work out this range and fail with a plain message if the axis range goes past it.
  2. Small counters have a floor. Strokes on closed shapes grow inward, so a small ring's counter shrinks as weight rises. Below a set minimum it has to change construction. At opsz 20 that may mean a hand-drawn master.
  3. Direction comes from role. Outer ink goes one way, and counters and knockouts go the other. The source file's directions are never trusted.
  4. Booleans per master are a fallback, not a method. Allow them only when the gate proves the masters match exactly. Flag them for review.
